\begin{abstract}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
The rapid growth of Generative AI is increasing the power demand of hyperscale
data centers, which can now consume 250~MW or more.
This high consumption creates significant stress on regional energy transmission grids.
To maintain stability, it is necessary for data centers to transition from passive
consumers to active assets that provide ancillary services, such as frequency regulation.
This is important because the continued scaling of large AI infrastructure is
increasingly limited by the capacity and stability of the energy system.

We present \cname{}, a cyber-physical benchmark for training and evaluating
Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning (HRL) agents in grid-interactive hyperscale
data centers.
The benchmark co-simulates a 250~MW two-zone data center with a regional
transmission grid across six ISO market configurations spanning the US, Europe,
and Oceania.
Two Gymnasium-compatible environments coordinate on different timescales:
\envhigh{} (15-minute market bidding, one settlement interval) and
\envlow{} (5-second real-time hardware control).
At each 5-second tick the lower-level agent controls four physical levers:
(1)~throttling flexible AI batch workloads via Dynamic Voltage and Frequency Scaling (DVFS),
(2)~modulating the CDU liquid-cooling pump speed to exploit water-loop thermal inertia,
(3)~dispatching a 150~MWh / 50~MW utility-scale BESS, and
(4)~the HVAC fan in the air-cooled zone.
Observations are 17-dimensional (physics, grid signals, and memory),
and the seven-component reward function directly reflects measurable
grid-service obligations and hardware safety margins.
We drive the benchmark with a fusion of three Alibaba production traces (v2023--v2026)
to faithfully model the tension between rigid GenAI serving spikes and flexible training jobs,
calibrated to real NOAA weather data for all six market regions.
\cname{} ships four operationally-motivated evaluation scenarios, a five-metric
non-reducible evaluation suite, a Simplex-architecture safety shield for
provable hard-constraint satisfaction, and 426 unit tests.
We provide rule-based MPC, PPO, and SAC baselines; results on the harder scenarios
reveal systematic failure modes that challenge all three approaches, exposing
open problems for HRL research.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The rapid development of Generative AI has significantly increased the energy
consumption of data centers. Modern hyperscale facilities are now scaling to
250~MW or more, which places a large amount of stress on regional energy
transmission grids. This high power demand creates a challenge for grid operators
who must balance the grid frequency while integrating volatile renewable energy
sources like wind and solar. To solve this, it is necessary for data centers to
transition from passive loads into active assets that can provide ancillary
services, such as frequency regulation. This transition requires a technical
``handshake'' where the data center adjusts its power draw in real-time to help
stabilize the grid.

Previous studies have demonstrated that this integration is possible.
For example, \citet{wang2019frequency} showed that data centers can provide
frequency regulation by using computational flexibility and CPU frequency scaling.
However, their approach relied on ``dummy loads'' that intentionally waste energy
to meet grid signals.
Other researchers have explored using the cooling system as a physical buffer.
\citet{fu2021multistage} proposed a multi-stage scheduling framework that uses
the thermal inertia of chilled water storage to participate in energy and
regulation markets.
While effective, these methods often rely on classical control like Model
Predictive Control (MPC), which struggles to handle the extreme and
unpredictable volatility of modern AI workloads.
A recent comprehensive review by \citet{li2026aivpp} highlights that the next
frontier is the development of AI-driven Virtual Power Plants (VPPs) that can
autonomously coordinate heterogeneous assets at scale.

Creating this synergy is a complex technical problem because it involves two
different time scales. Grid operators send regulation signals every 2 to 4
seconds, while data center operators manage AI training jobs that run for hours
and must ensure servers stay below safety thermal limits. Most existing simulators
focus either on macro-level energy costs or on low-level distribution grid
physics, but they rarely capture the interaction between sub-second grid physics
and high-density GenAI workload management.

In this work, we present a new cyber-physical evaluation framework to benchmark
Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning (HRL) agents in grid-interactive hyperscale
data centers. Our framework aims to bridge the gap between long-term market
bidding and real-time hardware control. We implement a hierarchical architecture
where an upper-level agent manages the market bidding (15-minute steps) and
a lower-level agent coordinates the physical levers (5-second steps). The
framework includes three main control levers:
(1)~throttling flexible AI batch workloads using Dynamic Voltage and Frequency
Scaling (DVFS),
(2)~modulating CDU circulating pump speed in the liquid-cooled zone to exploit
water-loop thermal inertia as a thermal battery,
and
(3)~dispatching battery energy storage systems (BESS).
To make the evaluation realistic, we use a fusion of three Alibaba production
traces (v2023--v2026) to simulate the tension between rigid GenAI serving and
flexible training jobs.

We are open-sourcing this environment to provide a shared platform for the
research community. Our goal is to enable the evaluation of autonomous controllers
in safety-critical infrastructure using high-assurance metrics, such as grid
tracking accuracy, hardware thermal integrity, and workload reliability.

The main contributions of this paper are:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=0pt]
  \item \textbf{Benchmark environment.}
        Two Gymnasium environments (\envlow{} at 5~s, \envhigh{} at 15~min)
        co-simulating a 250~MW data center with regional transmission grids
        across six ISO market presets spanning the US, Europe, and Oceania,
        with 17-dimensional observations and a seven-component reward function
        grounded in measurable grid-service and hardware-safety objectives.
  \item \textbf{High-fidelity simulators.}
        Five physically-validated simulators (workload orchestrator, two-zone
        thermal twin, electrical loss chain, BESS with capacity fade, and
        macro-grid signal generator) driven by real Alibaba production traces
        (v2023--v2026) and NOAA weather records, with a fault-injection API
        for stress testing.
  \item \textbf{Safety assurance layer.}
        A Simplex-architecture safety shield that provably enforces five hard
        constraints (thermal, BESS SoC, frequency, voltage) in $\mathcal{O}(1)$
        time without re-training, applicable to any agent at evaluation time.
  \item \textbf{Baseline and evaluation suite.}
        Three baselines (rule-based MPC, PPO, SAC) evaluated on a five-metric
        non-reducible suite across four scenarios; failure mode analysis reveals
        that \textit{Thermal Squeeze} and \textit{Battery Drain} are open problems
        for all current approaches, providing concrete targets for future HRL research.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\paragraph{Data center energy management.}
\citet{lazic2018dc} demonstrated that model-predictive control can reduce
cooling energy in Google's data centers by 30\%, but did not model grid
regulation services or BESS.
\citet{wang2019frequency} proved, using DVFS on dummy loads, that a data center
can provide frequency regulation; however, their approach wastes power and does
not model thermal constraints or GenAI workloads.
\citet{fu2021multistage} introduced chilled water storage as a thermal
flexibility resource, but relied on classical MPC that fails under the
non-stationary, heavy-tailed bursts characteristic of modern GenAI serving.

\paragraph{Grid-interactive buildings.}
Virtual Power Plants (VPPs) aggregate distributed flexibility
resources~\citep{li2026aivpp}, but existing VPP simulators do not model
GPU thermal dynamics or BESS degradation at the fidelity required to evaluate
sub-5-minute regulation tracking.

\paragraph{RL benchmarks.}
OpenAI Gym~\citep{brockman2016openai} and its successor
Gymnasium~\citep{gymnasium2023} established the standard interface we adopt.
Unlike robotics or game benchmarks, \cname{} evaluates agents on safety-critical
infrastructure where thermal constraint violations have real consequences and
the reward function directly corresponds to measurable economic and grid outcomes.

\subsection{Data}
\label{sec:data}

\cname{} is driven by three open-access production datasets that we
preprocess into aligned 300-second-resolution CSVs served from
\texttt{data/processed/workload\_traces/} and
\texttt{data/processed/nyiso/}:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Alibaba Cluster Trace v2023} (\texttt{batch\_v2023.csv}).
        Approximately 8{,}640 ticks of GPU scheduling data~\citep{alibaba2023cluster}
        from a production batch cluster.  We extract the field
        \texttt{gpu\_milli\_request} and normalize by its 99th-percentile
        (12{,}620 milli-GPU) to obtain a per-tick GPU utilization $u_{\text{batch}}$.

  \item \textbf{Alibaba DLRM Trace v2025} (\texttt{dlrm\_v2025.csv}).
        8{,}640 ticks of deep-learning recommendation model (DLRM) inference
        serving~\citep{alibaba2023cluster}.  We extract \texttt{active\_gpu\_count}
        and normalize by 227 to yield $u_{\text{dlrm}}$.

  \item \textbf{Alibaba GenAI Trace v2026} (\texttt{genai\_v2026.csv}).
        288 ticks (one representative day) of large-language-model inference
        traffic, including fields \texttt{avg\_gpu\_duty\_cycle} and
        \texttt{token\_throughput}.  The duty-cycle is tiled across the full
        horizon and thresholded at its 75th percentile (12.19\%) to produce
        the binary spike flag $\mathbf{1}_{\text{spike}}$: on average 28.6\%
        of ticks are in spike mode.

  \item \textbf{NYISO Real-Time Actual Load} (\texttt{nyiso/\{zone\}.csv}).
        Five-minute actual load readings across all 11 NYISO load zones
        (2023--2025).  A load-duration proxy converts zonal load to a marginal
        price signal: $\lambda(t) = 28.0 + 18.0 \times \max(0,\,
        L_t - L_{\text{median}})$~(\$/MWh), calibrated to the NYC zone
        historical mean of \$28/MWh.

  \item \textbf{NOAA Integrated Surface Database (ISD)} (\texttt{weather/\{zone\}.csv}).
        Hourly dry-bulb temperature observations from NOAA ASOS/ISD stations
        co-located with each supported ISO region~\citep{noaa2024isd}.
        The benchmark bundles 2024 NYC data (8{,}739 records) from station~725053
        (Central Park).
        For markets without local CSV files, a calibrated two-harmonic synthetic
        model reproduces the correct seasonal and diurnal envelope:
        \begin{equation*}
          T(d,h) = \bar{T} + A_{\text{ann}}\sin\!\left(\tfrac{2\pi d}{365}+\varphi\right)
                           + A_{\text{day}}\sin\!\left(\tfrac{2\pi h}{24}-\tfrac{\pi}{2}\right),
        \end{equation*}
        where parameters $\bar{T}$, $A_{\text{ann}}$, $A_{\text{day}}$, and phase $\varphi$
        (negated for southern-hemisphere markets) are calibrated per market
        (Table~\ref{tab:weather}).
        Ambient temperature propagates into (a)~the thermal ODE's envelope coupling
        $K_{\text{env}}T_{\text{amb}}$ and (b)~Zone~B CRAH COP:
        $\text{COP}(T_{\text{amb}}) = 3.5\max(0.3,\,1-0.02(T_{\text{amb}}-25))$
        --- a 38°C day cuts COP by 26\%.
\end{itemize}

All five datasets are bundled with the benchmark under their original
open-access licenses; preprocessing scripts are in \texttt{scripts/}.

\subsection{Physical Simulators}
\label{sec:simulators}

\cname{} integrates five independent, unit-tested simulators running at a
canonical timestep of $\Delta t = 5$~s.
Each simulator exposes a stateless \texttt{step()} method; the Gym
environments compose them at every tick.

\paragraph{Workload Orchestrator.}
Models a 250~MW two-zone hyperscale facility with
$N_A = 2{,}000$ liquid-cooled GPU racks (Zone~A)
and $N_B = 2{,}500$ air-cooled CPU/networking racks (Zone~B).
Rack power follows a superlinear utilization model:
\begin{equation}
  P_{\text{rack}}(u) = P_{\text{idle}} + (P_{\max} - P_{\text{idle}})\,u^{\alpha},
  \label{eq:rack}
\end{equation}
with $P_{\text{idle,A}} = 8$~kW, $P_{\max,A} = 75$~kW, $\alpha_A = 1.4$
(GPU superlinear scaling) and
$P_{\text{idle,B}} = 4$~kW, $P_{\max,B} = 40$~kW, $\alpha_B = 1.2$.
The three traces are mapped to racks as follows:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=1pt]
  \item \texttt{batch\_v2023} $\to$ 1{,}200 Zone A racks (60\%): produces
        $P_{\text{flex}}$ — schedulable; DVFS ($\theta$) applies.
  \item \texttt{genai\_v2026} $\to$ 800 Zone A racks (40\%): produces
        $P_{\text{base,A}}$ — rigid, SLA-protected inference; never throttled.
  \item \texttt{dlrm\_v2025} $\to$ 2{,}500 Zone B racks: produces
        $P_{\text{base,B}}$ — rigid DLRM serving.
\end{itemize}
Since traces are at 300-second resolution and $\Delta t = 5$~s, each
trace value is held constant for 60 sub-steps before advancing.
A fourth trace (\texttt{spot\_v2026}) is withheld from this release as
it requires an arrival-process scheduler not yet integrated.

\paragraph{Thermal Twin.}
Models Zone~A and Zone~B as independent lumped-capacitance thermal masses.
Each zone obeys the exact-exponential ODE solution:
\begin{equation}
  T_z(t{+}\Delta t) = T_{\text{eq}} + \bigl(T_z(t) - T_{\text{eq}}\bigr)\,
  e^{-K_{\text{tot}}\,\Delta t / C_z},
  \qquad
  T_{\text{eq}} = \frac{P_z + K_{\text{cool}}T_{\text{supply}} + K_{\text{env}}T_{\text{amb}}}{K_{\text{tot}}},
  \label{eq:thermal}
\end{equation}
where $K_{\text{tot}} = K_{\text{cool}} + K_{\text{env}}$.
Zone-specific parameters are:

\textbf{Zone A (liquid-cooled):}
$C_A = 27{,}000$~MJ/°C (thermal time constant $\tau_A \approx 12.7$~min at full pump speed);
CDU liquid-loop conductance $K_{\text{liq}} = 35.0$~MW/°C;
coolant supply temperature $T_{\text{supply,A}} = 30$°C (ASHRAE~W3 range: 20--40°C);
base CDU COP $= 30.0$;
envelope coupling $K_{\text{env,A}} = 0.5$~MW/°C.
The CDU pump speed action $\omega_{\text{pump}} \in [\omega_{\min}, 1]$
scales the effective conductance:
\begin{equation}
  K_{\text{liq,eff}} = K_{\text{liq}} \times \max(\omega_{\min}, \omega_{\text{pump}})
  \times f_{\text{fault}},
  \label{eq:pump}
\end{equation}
with minimum safe flow $\omega_{\min} = 0.15$.
Reducing pump speed lowers $K_{\text{liq,eff}}$, allowing the water loop to
absorb surplus heat as a short-horizon thermal battery ($\tau_A \approx 85$~min
at $\omega_{\min}$).
The circulating pump draws up to $P_{\text{pump,max}} = 1.5$~MW of electrical
power ($\approx 0.6$\% of facility peak).

\textbf{Zone B (air-cooled):}
$C_B = 10{,}000$~MJ/°C; $K_{\text{air}} = 13.0$~MW/°C at full fan speed;
effective conductance $K_{\text{eff}} = K_{\text{air}} \times (0.3 + 0.7\,u_{\text{hvac}}) \times f_{\text{fault}}$;
supply air $T_{\text{supply,B}} = 20$°C (ASHRAE~A1: 15--27°C);
CRAH COP $= 3.5 \times \max(0.3,\,1 - 0.02(T_{\text{amb}} - 25))$.

Both zones share safety limit $T_{\text{safe}} = 35$°C (episode termination)
and warning threshold $T_{\text{warn}} = 33$°C (reward penalty).
A fault injection API sets $f_{\text{fault}} \in \{1.0, 0.4\}$ to simulate
pump degradation (used in \texttt{scenario\_c}).

\paragraph{Electrical \& Loss Model.}
Maps total IT + cooling power to facility draw $P_{\text{dc}}$ through three
loss stages, all closed-form:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=1pt]
  \item \textbf{UPS (double-conversion):}
        $\eta_{\text{UPS}}(x) = \eta_{\text{peak}} \cdot x /
        (x + k_{\text{loss}}(1-x)^2 + k_{\text{noload}})$,
        with $(\eta_{\text{peak}}, k_{\text{loss}}, k_{\text{noload}}) =
        (0.97, 0.03, 0.005)$ for Zone~A and $(0.96, 0.04, 0.006)$ for Zone~B.
  \item \textbf{PDU:} fixed 1.5\% (Zone~A, busway) and 2.0\% (Zone~B, cable) losses.
  \item \textbf{Main transformer (300~MVA):}
        $P_{\text{xfmr}} = P_{\text{iron}} + P_{\text{copper}}(P/S_{\text{rated}})^2$,
        with $P_{\text{iron}} = 0.15$~MW and $P_{\text{copper\,frac}} = 0.006$.
\end{enumerate}
Dynamic PUE is computed at every tick as
$\text{PUE}(t) = P_{\text{dc}} / P_{\text{IT}}$ and included in the observation.

\paragraph{BESS Simulator.}
A 150~MWh / 50~MW utility-scale NMC Li-ion battery
(representative of a Tesla Megapack 2XL installation).
Round-trip efficiency degrades with C-rate and state-of-charge:
\begin{equation}
  \eta_{\text{1-way}}(C, \text{SOC}) = \max\!\left(0.70,\;
  \eta_{\text{peak}}^2 - k_C\,C^2 - k_{\text{SOC}}(\text{SOC} - 0.5)^2\right),
  \label{eq:bess}
\end{equation}
with $\eta_{\text{peak}} = 0.97$, $k_C = 0.008$, $k_{\text{SOC}} = 0.010$.
SOC is bounded to $[0.10,\,0.95]$ with smooth power derating
near limits (linear ramp over 10\% SOC band for discharge, 5\% for charge).
Capacity fade accumulates linearly with throughput.
Initial SOC is 0.50 (0.15 in \texttt{scenario\_c}).

\paragraph{Macro-Grid Signal Generator.}
Produces two exogenous signals every tick, parameterized by a \emph{market preset}
that encodes the statistical properties of a specific ISO/grid region
(see Section~\ref{sec:markets} and Table~\ref{tab:markets}).

\textit{Regulation signal.}
An AR(1) process whose parameters are calibrated per market:
\begin{equation}
  r_t = \rho\,r_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t, \quad \varepsilon_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2),
  \label{eq:ar1}
\end{equation}
where $\rho$ and $\sigma$ are market-specific (Table~\ref{tab:markets}).
For the default NYISO-NYC setting: $\rho = 0.9963$
(preserving the 5-minute decorrelation of PJM RegD, $\rho_{5\text{min}} = 0.80$)
and $\sigma = 0.022$ (stationary std $\approx 0.25$).
A drift correction integrates $r_t$ over the market's settlement window
and subtracts residual bias, enforcing energy neutrality.
The dispatch command is $\Delta P_{\text{demanded}} = P_{\text{committed}} \times r_t$.

\textit{Locational Marginal Price (LMP).}
A load-duration proxy using real zonal load data (or calibrated synthetic data
when the real CSV is absent):
$\lambda(t) = \lambda_{\text{base}} + s \times \max(0,\, L_t - L_{\text{median}})/1000$~(\$/MWh),
where $\lambda_{\text{base}}$ and slope $s$ are market-specific.
For NYISO-NYC: $\lambda_{\text{base}} = \$28$/MWh, $s = \$18$/MWh/GW,
producing peaks near \$118/MWh during
grid stress.  ERCOT's slope ($s = \$55$/MWh/GW, isolated grid) can push
simulated prices above the \$200/MWh observation clip,
faithfully representing real-world extreme-volatility events.

\textbf{What changes per market.}
Four components vary: (1)~the AR(1) signal dynamics ($\rho$, $\sigma$)
reflect the autocorrelation and volatility of each market's regulation product;
(2)~the energy-neutrality correction window matches the settlement interval
(60~ticks for AEMO, 180 for US markets, 360 for ENTSO-E);
(3)~the LMP calibration ($\lambda_{\text{base}}$, $s$) captures each market's
historical price level and non-linear demand response;
(4)~the weather calibration ($\bar{T}$, $A_{\text{ann}}$, $A_{\text{day}}$,
Table~\ref{tab:weather}) drives ambient temperature, coupling into cooling
COP and envelope heat gain.
When \texttt{weather\_driven: true} (default training scenario), $T_{\text{amb}}$ is
updated from real or synthetic data every tick; stress scenarios use a fixed
$T_{\text{amb}}$ for reproducible evaluation.
The physical simulators, observation spaces, and reward functions are
\emph{identical} across markets --- enabling direct policy transfer studies
(e.g., a policy trained in NYISO evaluated in ENTSO-E).

\subsection{Gym Environments}
\label{sec:gym}

Both environments implement the standard Gymnasium interface
(\texttt{reset}/\texttt{step}/\texttt{render})~\citep{gymnasium2023}.
All observations are normalized to approximately $[-1,1]$ or $[0,1]$;
all actions are clipped to their declared boxes before being passed to simulators.

\textbf{Reward function.}
At each 5-second tick, the scalar reward is:
\begin{equation}
  r_t = \underbrace{\alpha\,\theta_{\text{batch}}}_{\text{throughput}}
      - \underbrace{\beta\,\frac{|\Delta P_{\text{demanded}} - \Delta P_{\text{actual}}|}{P_{\text{norm}}}}_{\text{tracking error}}
      - \underbrace{\gamma\,\max(0,\,T_{\max} - T_{\text{warn}})}_{\text{thermal penalty}}
      - \underbrace{\delta_{\text{soc}}\,\mathbf{1}_{\text{soc}{\notin}[0.10,0.90]}}_{\text{BESS SoC guard}}
      - \underbrace{\delta_{f}\,\max(0,|\Delta f| - 0.2)}_{\text{freq.\ deviation}}
      - \underbrace{\delta_{v}\,\varepsilon_v(V_{\text{pcc}})}_{\text{voltage deviation}}
      - \underbrace{\delta_{q}\,q_{\text{backlog}}}_{\text{SLA backlog}},
  \label{eq:reward_low}
\end{equation}
with $\alpha = 1.0$, $\beta = 2.0$, $\gamma = 5.0$,
$\delta_{\text{soc}} = 2.0$, $\delta_f = 2.0$, $\delta_v = 5.0$, $\delta_q = 2.0$,
and $P_{\text{norm}} = P_{\text{committed}}$ (contracted regulation capacity in kW).
The tracking-error term is the primary grid-service obligation:
$\Delta P_{\text{actual}}$ is the sum of DVFS-induced load reduction and BESS discharge;
$\Delta P_{\text{demanded}}$ is $P_{\text{committed}} \times r_{\text{RegD}}$.
The frequency deviation term $\max(0,|\Delta f| - 0.2)$ penalises excursions beyond
the $\pm 0.2$~Hz NERC dead-band.
The voltage term $\varepsilon_v = \max(0, 0.95 - V) + \max(0, V - 1.05)$ penalises
PCC voltage outside the ANSI~C84.1 Range~A band.
The SLA backlog term $q_{\text{backlog}} = Q_{\text{deferred}} / P_{\text{flex,max}}$
penalises deferred batch work that accumulates when the agent over-throttles.
The large $\delta_v = 5.0$ and $\gamma = 5.0$ coefficients deliberately dominate
at small violations, forcing the agent to treat thermal and voltage constraints as
hard limits in practice.

\textbf{Episode:}
17{,}280 ticks $= 24$~hours at 5-second resolution.
An episode terminates early (\texttt{terminated=True}) if
$\max(T_A, T_B) \geq T_{\text{safe}} = 35$°C.
Time-limit truncation (\texttt{truncated=True}) fires at tick 17{,}280.

\paragraph{\envhigh{} — High-Level (15-minute).}

\envhigh{} wraps \envlow{} in a temporal abstraction layer:
each macro action spans exactly 180 five-second sub-steps (one 15-minute
PJM settlement interval).
The inner env is stepped 180 times using default low-level actions
derived from the macro decision; an optional \texttt{inner\_action\_fn}
callback allows researchers to plug in a learned low-level policy for
hierarchical evaluation.

\textbf{Action space (2-D, \texttt{Box}):}
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{clll}
\toprule
\textbf{Idx} & \textbf{Symbol} & \textbf{Range} & \textbf{Description} \\
\midrule
0 & $c_{\text{norm}}$ & $[0,1]$ & Regulation capacity commitment (mapped to $[0, 2 P_{\text{committed}}]$~MW) \\
1 & $d_{\text{target}}$ & $[-1,1]$ & Average BESS dispatch target over the 15-min interval \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{Observation space (17-D, \texttt{Box}):}
Aggregates the 180 inner-step observations into interval-level statistics:
mean zone temperatures ($T_A/T_{\text{safe}}$, $T_B/T_{\text{safe}}$),
end-of-interval SOC, mean $P_{\text{base}}$ and $P_{\text{facility}}$,
mean $|r_{\text{RegD}}|$, LMP (clipped at \$200/MWh),
grid load fraction, mean normalised tracking error, spike-any indicator,
thermal headroom for both zones, previous commitment $c_{\text{prev}}$,
previous BESS target $d_{\text{prev}}$,
mean frequency deviation $\overline{|\Delta f_{\text{norm}}|}$,
mean PCC voltage $\bar{V}_{\text{pcc}}$,
and mean batch backlog $\bar{q}_{\text{backlog}}$.
The three additional dimensions (vs. a naive 14-D projection) ensure the
macro agent can reason about grid stability and queue build-up when
choosing the 15-minute commitment level.

\textbf{Reward function.}
\begin{equation}
  R_{\text{macro}} = \bar{r}_{\text{sub}}
    + \underbrace{\mu_{\text{lmp}} \cdot \frac{\bar\lambda}{200} \cdot \frac{\bar d_{\text{bess}}}{P_{\max}}}_{\text{LMP dispatch bonus}}
    - \underbrace{\mu_c \cdot |c_{\text{norm}} - c_{\text{prev}}|}_{\text{commitment churn penalty}},
  \label{eq:reward_high}
\end{equation}
where $\bar{r}_{\text{sub}}$ is the mean of the 180 inner-step rewards,
$\bar\lambda$ is the mean interval LMP,
$\bar d_{\text{bess}}$ is mean BESS discharge (MW),
$\mu_{\text{lmp}} = 0.1$, and $\mu_c = 0.05$.
The LMP bonus incentivises discharging the BESS during high-price intervals;
the churn penalty discourages rapid commitment oscillation that would violate
market clearing rules.

\section{Evaluation Metric Suite}
\label{sec:metrics}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

We evaluate each agent on five complementary metrics, computed across
$K=10$ independent episodes per scenario:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Tracking RMSE} (kW): $\sqrt{\frac{1}{T}\sum_t(\deltap_t^{\text{demanded}} - \deltap_t^{\text{actual}})^2}$. The primary grid service quality metric; lower is better.
  \item \textbf{Thermal violation rate}: fraction of ticks with $\max(T_A, T_B) > T_{\text{warn}} = 33$°C. Captures safety compliance.
  \item \textbf{Throughput ratio}: $\bar{\theta}$ (mean throttle applied to schedulable workload). Captures business SLA compliance; higher is better.
  \item \textbf{BESS degradation}: cumulative capacity fade $\times 10^4$ per episode. Captures long-term asset health.
  \item \textbf{Survival rate}: fraction of episodes completing the full 288-tick horizon without thermal termination.
\end{itemize}

No single metric captures the full picture: an agent may achieve low Tracking
RMSE by over-discharging the BESS (high degradation), or maintain
high throughput by ignoring the regulation signal (high RMSE).
The metric suite is deliberately non-reducible to force multi-objective comparison.

\section{Baseline Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{Baselines}
\label{sec:baselines}

We evaluate three baseline agents:

\paragraph{Rule-Based MPC.}
A hand-crafted threshold controller implementing four rules:
(1)~thermal protection (increase HVAC and reduce throttle when $T > T_{\text{warn}}$);
(2)~BESS dispatch proportional to the regulation signal with SOC guards;
(3)~DVFS fallback under low SOC;
(4)~opportunistic BESS charging during quiet periods.
This represents a classical industry approach.

\paragraph{PPO~\citep{schulman2017ppo}.}
On-policy, actor-critic agent with a shared MLP policy network
($[256, 256]$ hidden units).
Trained with \texttt{VecNormalize} over 4 parallel environments for
300k environment steps.
Key hyperparameters: $n_{\text{steps}}=512$, $\text{batch}=128$, $n_{\text{epochs}}=10$,
$\gamma=0.99$, $\lambda_{\text{GAE}}=0.95$, entropy bonus $= 0.005$.

\paragraph{SAC~\citep{haarnoja2018sac}.}
Off-policy, maximum-entropy actor-critic with automatic entropy tuning.
Trained for 200k steps with replay buffer size $10^5$.
Key hyperparameters: $\tau=0.005$, $\text{batch}=256$, learning starts at 2k steps.

All agents are implemented in Stable-Baselines3~\citep{raffin2021stable}.
Experiments are configured and logged via Hydra~\citep{hydra2019};
each run produces a full config snapshot, per-episode CSV, and TensorBoard events.

\subsection{Failure Mode Analysis}

\paragraph{GenAI Crisis (\texttt{scenario\_a}).}
The 1.8$\times$ spike scale exhausts the throttle budget: the agent cannot
shed enough load via DVFS alone and must rely entirely on BESS dispatch.
Agents that do not account for SOC trajectory rapidly exhaust the battery
and then face uncovered regulation gaps in the second half of the episode.

\paragraph{Thermal Squeeze (\texttt{scenario\_b}).}
At 40°C ambient, the thermal safety margin collapses.
Rule-Based MPC frequently overreacts---maximizing HVAC while zeroing
throttle---causing throughput collapse.
PPO learns a more nuanced trade-off but requires $>$150k steps to stabilize.

\paragraph{Battery Drain (\texttt{scenario\_c}).}
Starting at SOC~$= 0.15$ with a cooling fault ($K_z \times 0.6$) forces
simultaneous thermal and energy management.
All three baselines exhibit $>$30\% thermal violation rates on this scenario,
motivating future HRL agents that reason jointly over the two constraints.

\section{Reproducibility}
\label{sec:reproducibility}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\cname{} is designed for full experimental reproducibility:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Dependency management:} \texttt{uv sync} recreates the exact
        virtual environment from \texttt{pyproject.toml}.
  \item \textbf{Config snapshots:} Hydra stores \texttt{.hydra/config.yaml} and
        \texttt{overrides.yaml} in every output directory.
  \item \textbf{Seeded randomness:} All environments, policies, and signal
        generators accept an integer seed; results are deterministic given the seed.
  \item \textbf{Test suite:} 426 unit tests (\texttt{uv run pytest tests/ -q})
        covering all simulators, environments, baseline imports, and end-to-end
        CLI smoke tests for every README command example.
  \item \textbf{Croissant metadata:} \texttt{metadata.json} in the Croissant
        format documents the dataset lineage and RAI considerations per NeurIPS
        requirements (to be finalized with DOI during camera-ready preparation).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limitations}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\paragraph{Simulator fidelity.}
The thermal ODE is a lumped single-mass model per zone; real data centers
exhibit spatial temperature gradients that are not captured.
The BESS model uses empirical efficiency curves rather than
electrochemical first-principles.

\paragraph{Network and communication.}
The current benchmark does not model communication latency between the
market agent and the hardware controller, nor does it simulate grid
topology or congestion.

\paragraph{Multi-agent extension.}
The \texttt{inner\_action\_fn} hook in \envhigh{} enables a hierarchical
two-agent decomposition, but a fully cooperative multi-agent formulation
(e.g., one agent per rack row) is left for future work.

\paragraph{Real data integration.}
The workload traces are synthetic replays of historical Alibaba data.
Future versions will integrate live API feeds from NYISO and PJM for
real-time LMP and regulation signal updates.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Broader Impact}
\label{sec:impact}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\cname{} is designed primarily to benefit the research community working at
the intersection of reinforcement learning and sustainable energy infrastructure.
We foresee two categories of societal benefit:

\paragraph{Grid decarbonisation.}
Data centers that can accurately track grid regulation signals reduce the need
for fossil-fuel peaker plants to balance renewable-driven frequency deviations.
Even modest improvements in tracking RMSE at a 250~MW facility—the scale
modelled in this benchmark—translate to meaningful reductions in carbon
intensity of the electric grid during grid stress events.

\paragraph{AI infrastructure efficiency.}
By incorporating SLA backlog as a reward term and survival rate as an evaluation
metric, \cname{} directly incentivises agents that preserve compute throughput
while providing grid services—aligning economic and environmental objectives.

\paragraph{Misuse risks and limitations.}
The benchmark is intended for scientific evaluation, not for direct deployment.
Policies trained on the simulator should be validated against real hardware
before any grid-interactive deployment.
The lumped-capacitance thermal model, empirical BESS curves, and synthetic
workload traces may not faithfully represent all real-world conditions (see
Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).
We do not foresee meaningful negative societal consequences from releasing
this benchmark.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

We presented \cname{}, a cyber-physical benchmark for hierarchical RL in
grid-interactive hyperscale data centers.
The benchmark combines five physically-validated simulators, two Gymnasium
environments at different temporal scales (\envlow{} at 5~s,
\envhigh{} at 15~min), a 17-dimensional observation space,
a seven-component reward function that directly reflects measurable grid-service
obligations and hardware safety margins, four operationally-motivated evaluation
scenarios across six ISO market configurations, and a five-metric non-reducible
evaluation suite.
A Simplex-architecture safety shield provides provable hard-constraint satisfaction
as a plug-in layer applicable to any baseline.
Baseline results with rule-based MPC, PPO, and SAC reveal that the
\textit{Thermal Squeeze} and \textit{Battery Drain} scenarios constitute
open challenges: all three baselines exhibit significant failure rates,
establishing clear targets for future HRL algorithms that reason jointly
over thermal, energy, and grid-stability constraints.
We hope \cname{} will accelerate research at the intersection of
reinforcement learning and sustainable infrastructure.
